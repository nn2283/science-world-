⚛ Physics

Discover how light behaves.

Light

Reflection

Reflection happens when light bounces off a surface.

Example: You see your reflection in a mirror because light reflects from the mirror and travels to your eyes.

Remember: The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
Light

Refraction

Refraction is the change in direction of light when it passes from one material into another because its speed changes.

Example: A straw in a glass of water can look bent because light changes direction as it moves between water and air.

Remember: Refraction happens when light travels between materials, such as air and water.
Magnetism

What is magnetism?

Magnetism is a force that can attract or repel certain materials and other magnets. A magnet has two poles: a north pole (N) and a south pole (S).

The area around a magnet where magnetic force acts is called its magnetic field. The field is strongest near the poles.

Remember: Opposite poles attract (N and S). Like poles repel (N and N, or S and S).
Magnetic materials

What do magnets attract?

Magnets strongly attract some materials, including iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. They do not attract every metal: aluminium and copper are not normally attracted to an ordinary magnet.

  • Magnetic: iron nails and many steel objects.
  • Not usually magnetic: wood, plastic, glass, copper and aluminium.

A compass contains a small magnetised needle. It turns to line up with Earth's magnetic field, so its north-seeking end points roughly towards geographic north.

Electromagnets

Making a magnet with electricity

An electromagnet is made when an electric current flows through a coil of wire. Putting an iron core inside the coil makes the magnet stronger.

  • Switch the current on: the electromagnet works.
  • Switch the current off: most of its magnetism disappears.
  • More turns of wire or a larger current can make it stronger (within safe limits).

Electromagnets are used in devices such as scrapyard lifting cranes, electric bells and some motors.

Key difference: A permanent magnet stays magnetic; an electromagnet can be switched on and off.

🫁 Biology

Explore the respiratory system and how gas exchange works.

Respiratory system

Diagram of the lungs

This simplified diagram shows the main airways inside the lungs.

Air pathways

Bronchi and bronchioles

  • Trachea: The windpipe that carries air towards the lungs.
  • Bronchi: The two main branches from the trachea, one leading to each lung.
  • Bronchioles: Smaller branching air tubes inside the lungs.
  • Alveoli: Tiny air sacs at the ends of bronchioles.
Gas exchange

How gas exchange works

Gas exchange happens in the alveoli.

  • Oxygen moves from the air in the alveoli into the blood.
  • Carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the alveoli.
  • Carbon dioxide is then breathed out.
Remember: Oxygen goes into the blood; carbon dioxide leaves the blood.
Alveoli

Why are alveoli useful?

Alveoli have thin walls and a large combined surface area. They are surrounded by tiny blood vessels called capillaries, helping gases move between the air and blood.

Movement of particles

Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration.

  • Particles move randomly all the time.
  • Overall, more particles move from the crowded area to the less crowded area.
  • Diffusion continues until particles are spread more evenly.

In the lungs: Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli, where its concentration is higher, into the blood, where its concentration is lower. Carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction.

Remember: Diffusion moves particles down a concentration gradient — from high concentration to low concentration.
Breathing muscles

The diaphragm

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped muscle underneath the lungs. It helps air move into and out of the lungs.

  • Breathing in: The diaphragm contracts, flattens and moves down. The chest cavity gets bigger, so air moves into the lungs.
  • Breathing out: The diaphragm relaxes and moves up into its dome shape. The chest cavity gets smaller, helping push air out.
Remember: Diaphragm down = breathe in. Diaphragm up = breathe out (during normal, relaxed breathing).
Breathing muscles

Intercostal muscles

Intercostal muscles are found between the ribs. They help move the rib cage during breathing.

  • Breathing in: The external intercostal muscles contract, lifting the ribs up and out.
  • Breathing out normally: These muscles relax, and the ribs move down and in.

The diaphragm and intercostal muscles work together to change the size of the chest cavity.

Blood

What is in our blood?

Blood is made of four main components. Each one has a different job:

  • Red blood cells: These contain haemoglobin, a red protein that carries oxygen from the lungs to the body. They have a flattened, disc-like shape to help them carry oxygen.
  • White blood cells: These are part of the immune system. They help the body fight germs, such as bacteria and viruses.
  • Platelets: These are tiny cell fragments that help blood clot and stop bleeding when a blood vessel is damaged.
  • Plasma: This is the pale-yellow liquid part of blood. It is mostly water and carries the blood cells, nutrients, hormones, salts, carbon dioxide and waste products around the body.

What is blood made up of?

In a typical blood sample, plasma makes up about 55% of the volume. Red blood cells make up most of the other 45%; white blood cells and platelets make up a very small amount.

Remember: Plasma is the liquid. Red blood cells carry oxygen. White blood cells fight infection. Platelets help blood clot.
Gas transport

How does blood carry gases?

  • Oxygen moves from the alveoli into the blood and attaches to haemoglobin in red blood cells.
  • The blood carries oxygen to body cells, where it is used for respiration.
  • Carbon dioxide made by body cells travels in the blood back to the lungs. Most is carried as bicarbonate dissolved in the plasma.
  • At the lungs, carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the alveoli and is breathed out.
Remember: Red blood cells carry most oxygen. Plasma carries many dissolved substances, including much of the carbon dioxide.

  • pH below 7: Acidic (0–6).
  • pH 7: Neutral.
  • pH above 7: Alkaline (8–14).

Note: The lower the pH, the more acidic a solution is. The higher the pH, the more alkaline it is.

Acids

What are acids?

Acids are substances that produce hydrogen ions in water. Many acids have a pH below 7.

Examples include lemon juice and vinegar.

Alkalis

What are alkalis?

Alkalis are bases that dissolve in water and produce hydroxide ions. They have a pH above 7.

Examples include baking soda solution and soap solution.

Chemical reaction

Neutralisation

Neutralisation happens when an acid reacts with an alkali (a soluble base).

  1. An acid and an alkali are mixed.
  2. Hydrogen ions from the acid react with hydroxide ions from the alkali to make water.
  3. The remaining ions form a salt.
  4. If the correct amounts are mixed, the solution becomes neutral, around pH 7.
Acid + Alkali → Salt + Water

What colour does the acid become? There is no single colour for every acid. The colour depends on the indicator used. With universal indicator, an acidic solution is red, orange or yellow. As it is neutralised towards pH 7, it changes towards green.

With litmus, acid turns blue litmus red; a neutral solution does not turn either litmus paper red or blue.

🚀 Space Facts

A few amazing facts about our Solar System and the universe.

The Sun

Our nearest star

The Sun is a star at the centre of our Solar System. It is so far away that its light takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.

The planets

Jupiter is the biggest

Jupiter is the largest planet in our Solar System. More than 1,300 Earths could fit inside it by volume.

Saturn

A planet with rings

Saturn's rings are made mostly of pieces of ice and rock. They stretch out around the planet and are very wide.

Earth

Our home planet

Earth takes about 365.25 days to travel once around the Sun. That extra quarter-day is why we have a leap year every four years (with a few calendar exceptions).

The Moon

One familiar face

The Moon takes about the same amount of time to spin once as it takes to orbit Earth. That is why we usually see the same side of the Moon.

The universe

It's incredibly old

Scientists estimate that the universe is about 13.8 billion years old. It contains billions of galaxies.

Space question: Which planet is the largest in our Solar System? Answer: Jupiter.
